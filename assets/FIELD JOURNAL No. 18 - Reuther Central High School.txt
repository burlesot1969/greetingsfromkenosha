FIELD JOURNAL No. 18: Reuther Central High School
How a visionary superintendent, a Chicago architect, and a daring 1925 chimney climb brought a true palace of learning to Kenosha’s working-class kids.
By Todd Burleson
November 4, 2026
Link: https://whereimaginationtakesflight.substack.com/p/field-journal-no-18-reuther-central

If you take a stroll down 57th Street on a late autumn afternoon, the low sun washes over an entire city block of carved Indiana gray limestone. Tucked near the edge of the parking lot is a quiet detail you might miss if you aren’t looking closely. A solitary stone marker sits in the grass, salvaged from the original 1890 Kenosha High School building that stood here before the city cleared space for Civic Center Park. Just past it, the grand 1926 building at 913 57th Street anchors the south edge of the green. Its carved stonework holds up remarkably well. You can spot the soft weathering where a century of rain has brushed against the heavy stone, right alongside freshly repainted metal panels framing the front doors.

This block has worn a few different names. When it opened in 1926, it was the new Kenosha High School, stretching across a full square block. Later on, folks knew it as Central High, then Mary D. Bradford High School to honor the visionary superintendent, and today it houses the students of Reuther Central High School and the Harborside Academy.

Back in 1925, Kenosha was a loud factory town defined by screeching rail lines, heavy machinery, and coal smoke. Why build such a magnificent classical sanctuary in the middle of a wagon-making hub?

Superintendent Mary D. Bradford argued that working-class children deserved the exact same dignity and beauty in their education as anyone else. She brought in school design specialist Charles D. Chubb to turn that demand into stone. Picture a fifteen-year-old kid in 1925 whose dad spent all day breathing in sawdust and axle grease at Bain Wagon Works. Walking up those grand steps and passing beneath those massive carved Corinthian columns must have felt like stepping into a bigger world. It was a quiet promise that anything was possible.

Building on this scale took immense labor and drastic city planning. To make space for the new school and the courthouse across the way, crews cleared away several blocks of existing homes and buildings. By 1925, construction workers were setting the final limestone blocks into place. To document the changing grid, local photographer C. Wilkins Beemer pulled off a terrifying feat.

He scaled the raw, unfinished chimney of the heating plant. Decades before anyone dreamed of drones, he had the stubborn grit to haul heavy film camera gear up that tall brick stack. I can almost feel my own legs shaking just thinking about it. With coal dust in his lungs and raw hands gripping the masonry, he captured breathtaking views of our downtown grid and harbor. He would have made a fantastic mountaineer.

Standing on the sidewalk and framing this building in my iPhone viewfinder, I tried to capture that same stubborn permanence. Translating the structure into a digital vintage-style postcard meant stripping away the modern noise to focus on the sharp lines and tall columns that force you to stop and look. Watching old film clips of stonemasons at work reminds you of the sheer physical toll required to carve all that Indiana limestone.

Even after a hundred Wisconsin winters and generations of students pouring through its doors, the limestone survives intact. Mary D. Bradford’s vision of public education still anchors the block, welcoming anyone who walks up 57th Street.
