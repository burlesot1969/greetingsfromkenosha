FIELD JOURNAL No. 24: The Rhode Center for the Arts
The Immigrant Builder, the Wizard of Oz, and the Volunteers at 514 56th Street
By Todd Burleson
November 25, 2026
Link: https://whereimaginationtakesflight.substack.com/p/field-journal-no-24-the-rhode-center

I walk past 514 56th Street several times a day, most days. Living right around the corner, that projecting marquee is part of my daily sidewalk rhythm. I am still hunting for the exact right camera angle on it to frame and hang in our apartment. Even if you only catch the front in passing, the building holds down the whole block between 5th and 6th Avenues.

Inside, the craftsmanship runs deeper than most passersby realize. During a visit to the Pollard Gallery directly next door, a local artist working as a docent showed us around. She pointed out the antique Moorish tilework along the walls, the ceramic benches covered in scenes from Don Quixote, and the Pearlman crystal chandeliers designed by Rapp & Rapp. Then she opened a sneaky side door leading straight into the theater lobby, and the exact same tile and frieze work carried right across the threshold.

That physical connection makes sense, because today’s Rhode Center for the Arts is actually the third theater built on this single lot. It started in 1886 when a German immigrant named Peter Rhode Sr. bought the hotel just east of here and renamed it the Commercial Hotel. Four years later, he bought the lot next door and put up a 1,000-seat opera house that opened in August 1891.

Less than five years later, a February 1896 fire burned the whole thing to the ground. The damage came to $40,000, and Rhode only had $20,000 in insurance. He ate half the loss out of his own pocket and rebuilt on the exact same footprint. By 1901, the second opera house was already showing early moving pictures alongside live stage acts.

Running that place was a family affair. Peter Sr. handed management over to his son Joseph, who booked big names like George M. Cohan, John Philip Sousa, William Howard Taft, and Amelia Earhart. Another son, Peter Jr., worked the streets as Kenosha’s billposter. They even survived a January 1904 city inspection that padlocked the doors until the family installed a steel curtain and upgraded the fireproofing.

Twenty years later, the block changed again. In 1924, Milwaukee’s Saxe Amusement Co. bought the property, tore down the second opera house in 1926, and hired Chicago theater architects Rapp & Rapp to build an 1,800-seat movie palace. When the Gateway Theatre opened on December 29, 1927, crowds walked into an air-conditioned hall with the largest stage in Wisconsin outside the Milwaukee Auditorium. Featured organist Leonard M. Salvo, described by a fellow Chicago musician as “very Bohemian,” played a $50,000 Barton pipe organ before moving on to play for the Little Orphan Annie radio broadcasts.

Twelve years after opening night, Hollywood quietly tested its biggest gamble right here. MGM had poured millions into Technicolor for The Wizard of Oz and worried the musical might flop. Before its official August 15 Hollywood debut, the studio slipped the film into a handful of small Midwestern theaters to see how real audiences reacted. Whether you go by the August 11 date tracked by Oz historians or the August 13 local date honored down the street at Oz Plaza, Kenosha matinee crowds watched Dorothy step into color days before Los Angeles ever saw it.

As movies and shopping drifted away from downtown after World War II, the building fought to stay alive. It reopened as the remodeled Lake Theatre in 1963 and split into the twinned Lake 1 & 2 in 1976. Local families packed the weekend matinees and neighborhood teenagers worked the aisles as ushers until March 1984. After a final double feature of Paul Newman’s Harry & Son and Against All Odds, the doors locked, shutting down the last operating movie house in downtown Kenosha.

By December 1987, the empty building was headed for the wrecking ball. That is when Judy Rossow stepped in. Before serving on the Kenosha County Board and chairing the Lakeshore Business Improvement District, Judy and her husband, Gordy, owned Kenosha’s first quick-print shop. As a downtown shop owner, she knew a dark marquee meant a dead block, so she teamed up with BID manager Bob Irwin and the Rhode heirs to stop the demolition.

They found the right tenants in the Lakeside Players, a local community theater group founded in 1973 that badly needed a permanent stage. The troupe moved in during the spring of 1988, bought the building outright in September 1989, and renamed it the Rhode Center for the Arts to honor Peter Rhode’s 1891 original. For over thirty years, local actors, set builders, and volunteers have kept the roof patched and the heat running through fundraisers like “Bowls and Books,” which Judy helped start around 2000, and 24-hour theater marathons.

When you pack live audiences, touring stagehands, and neighborhood volunteers into one spot for over a century, the place picks up a few lingering regulars. Stage crews still talk about the Lavender Lady, a misty figure paired with the sudden smell of fresh lavender in the dressing room mirrors. Others report phantom piano notes and footsteps pacing across an empty stage. During an October 1996 performance of the murder mystery Post-Mortem, sudden blasts of cold air and flickering lights even lined up right on cue with an on-stage séance scene.

Today, downtown Kenosha still has three surviving historic theaters (the Orpheum, the Kenosha, and the Rhode), and 514 56th Street is the only one open and running. In 2017, the community named the 300-capacity entrance the Judy Rossow Grand Lobby. It connects an immigrant hotel owner, a quick-print shop operator, and the neighbors who refused to let the curtain stay down. I plan to catch another show there soon, walking right back past that shared gallery tilework where a century of Kenosha still shows up to work.
