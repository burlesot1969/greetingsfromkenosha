FIELD JOURNAL No. 19: The 1933 Post Office
Six Pairs of Doric Columns and the Enclosure of Civic Center Plaza
By Todd Burleson
November 10, 2026
Link: https://whereimaginationtakesflight.substack.com/p/field-journal-no-19-the-1933-post

Some buildings politely share the downtown grid. The 1933 Post Office at 5605 Sheridan Road commands it.

Catch the early light hitting the east side of Civic Center Plaza, and the first thing that demands your attention is the sheer physical weight of those six paired groups of colossal Doric columns. Civic anchor: It projects a quiet, unshakeable authority. I am no architect, and I don’t know the specific quarry that produced the rock, but the imposing scale is impossible to miss. Designed as a one-story structure, its soaring interior ceilings give it the heavy presence of a two-story monument.

I spent a good twenty minutes pacing the pavement across the street, trying to figure out how to translate that gravity into this week’s poster. It is funny how distilling a massive Neo-Classical building down to flat, WPA-style color blocks changes your perspective. It forces you to ignore the modern street noise and the cars driving past. You stop seeing a functioning post office and start seeing the raw, load-bearing geometry that anchors the block. Behind that pristine facade lies a story rooted in the sheer, overwhelming manpower of industrial Kenosha.

To understand why this massive stone fortress guards the plaza, you have to picture Kenosha in the 1920s. It was a loud factory town defined by screeching rail lines, heavy machinery, and coal smoke. Fueled by local industries like Simmons Bedding and Nash Motors, thousands of new factory workers poured into the city.

All those hands generated a staggering, relentless paper trail. Paychecks, catalogs, and letters sent back home created a logistical crisis. The sheer volume of mail brought the city’s original 1908 federal post office to its knees. The paper trail of a booming industrial town caused the facility to outgrow its own usefulness in barely twenty years.

At the same time, Kenosha hired famed urban planner Harland Bartholomew to design a grand public plaza. He envisioned a symmetrical, neoclassical park system mirroring the civic spaces of Washington, D.C. But completing that ten-year dream required dealing with a massive problem. The overwhelmed 1908 post office was sitting right in the middle of his proposed parkway.

Rather than trying to expand the old building, the federal government decided to construct a sprawling new post office to anchor the east edge of the plaza. They brought in federal architect George Daidy to design a $150,000 facility facing Sheridan Road. By 1933, much of the nation was shifting toward Art Deco styles, but Daidy intentionally kept to a restrained Neo-Classical Revival design. He wanted his new post office to perfectly harmonize with the heavy load-bearing masonry of Joseph Lindl’s 1925 Courthouse and Charles D. Chubb’s 1926 High School across the park.

When Daidy’s grand, Doric-columned post office finally opened its doors, it acted as a massive relief valve for the city’s working class. Completing this shiny new federal anchor triggered one of the most absurd engineering stunts of the Great Depression.

Because the new building could handle the city’s massive mail volume, the government deeded the old Beaux-Arts building to the City of Kenosha for exactly one dollar. As we explored in Field Journal No. 01, contractors then used heavy jack screws and a sprawling network of heavy timbers to lift the massive stone and brick building. They dragged all 1,500 tons of that original building west across the frozen mud of the plaza to become the Kenosha Public Museum.

Today, 5605 Sheridan Road remains the anchor of the east block. It continues to serve as an active downtown postal facility. It handles the daily mail of modern Kenoshans while standing as an essential architectural wall for the Civic Center Historic District. We walk past these buildings every day, rarely thinking about the sheer grit it took to place them there. The factory booms of the 1920s have faded, but the stone monument built to handle their correspondence survives intact.
