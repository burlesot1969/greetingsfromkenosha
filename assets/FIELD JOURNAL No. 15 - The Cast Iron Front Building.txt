FIELD JOURNAL No. 15: The Cast Iron Front Building
Mass-Produced Iron, Schoolyard Dreams, and the Roar of the Rails
By Todd Burleson
October 27, 2026
Link: https://whereimaginationtakesflight.substack.com/p/field-journal-no-15-the-cast-iron

If you walk east down 55th Street on a crisp Midwestern morning, crossing toward 11th Avenue, the heavy rhythm of the city is impossible to ignore. The thick scent of diesel locomotive engines drifts off the Metra platform, cutting through the steady mechanical hum of the nearby police, jail, and courthouse complex.

Depending on the hour, the sharp ring of a streetcar bell or the clatter of its wheels along the track joins the deep, metallic screech of departing commuter trains. Those heavy rail sounds reverberate off the massive concrete embankment wall along 13th Avenue. Constructed in the 1920s, this elevated rail barrier severed Pearl Street from the downtown grid. It unintentionally preserved a tiny, four-building commercial corridor as a quiet island anchored in time.

Walk to the eastern end of the block. You will find the oldest commercial survivor on the street. Built in 1868, the structure at 1100 to 1106 55th Street acts as an industrial sentinel guarding the rail line. Today, the upper floors hold massive 2,200-square-foot Chicago-style residential lofts, while local practices like Generational Cycles LLC occupy the ground level.

Up above, the flat roof over the single-story western annex serves a pragmatic modern purpose: rather than utilizing traditional hanging balconies, the stepped rooflines create quiet, private walk-out terraces. It is a simple, functional evolution of the 19th-century footprint. But step inside and look down at the foundation, where raising the street level in the 20th century tucked away the original lower level, leaving blocked brick arches still visible in the dark interior basement today.

How did post-Civil War shopkeepers afford such intricate, heavy metalwork decades before the railroads brought widespread wealth to the harbor?

The answer lies in the harsh realities of 19th-century mass production. In the late 1860s, carving a decorative stone front required painstaking hand-chiseling by master stonemasons. Harbor merchants could not afford that luxury. Instead, they relied on heavy iron foundries. Workers poured molten iron into molds to cast slender metal columns framing the storefronts and strong window arches overhead. They shipped these massive components by rail to be assembled on-site in the freezing mud of early Kenosha.

Holding my iPhone still in the cold morning light, I frame the deep shadows cast by the stepped rooflines, the textured Cream City brick, and the stacked brick patterns woven across the facade. Translating this image into flat, saturated WPA-style vector art reveals the strong geometry of the block. Structural strength: Because cast iron possessed immense load-bearing capacity, slender metal columns framing the storefronts replaced thick masonry walls. Merchants installed massive plate-glass windows to flood their sales floors with natural light.

Those original glass panes showcased dry goods and groceries to switchmen covered in coal dust stepping off the trains. Decades later, that same rugged aesthetic captivated a Reuther High School teenager named Trevor Brooks. He spent his class breaks wandering down 55th Street, staring at the stark, unadorned Cream City brick and the heavy storefronts.

Trevor eventually opened Kenosha Coffee Roasters inside 1106 55th Street. He crafted signature blends like “Off The Rails” in direct homage to the Metra passenger trains that vibrated the heavy floorboards every hour. Visiting that roastery just a couple of months before it suddenly closed in 2026 was incredible. The interior was a masterclass in local design, blending Art Deco accents with Frank Lloyd Wright-inspired craft. Hand-lettering adorned the walls alongside framed historic photos of Kenosha police officers, a deliberate nod to the nearby county courthouse and jail. It is a beautiful space.

A quiet rumor suggests it might reopen. I certainly hope it does. I only wish I could have grabbed a fresh cup during my own years commuting to work and school.

Like its neighbors, this cast-iron pioneer survived postwar blight and near demolition in the late 1980s. A dedicated rescue by local preservationists and private developers in the early 1990s saved all three iron-and-brick structures. Their efforts culminated in the 1998 designation of the Pearl Street Historic District.

Together, these three dispatches complete the Pearl Street Island Trilogy:
No. 13 (The Maple House): The 1880 Schlitz tied-house saloon of Telephone No. 77, Prohibition speakeasies, and the 1922 Egg Brawl.
No. 14 (The Winberg Building): The 1907 Scandinavian grocery anchor, home to A. Emin and the Sammy Man ice cream fleet.
No. 15 (The Cast Iron Front Building): The 1868 industrial iron pioneer bridging 19th-century shopkeepers with 21st-century loft dwellers.
