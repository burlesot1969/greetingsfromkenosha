FIELD JOURNAL No. 17: The Kenosha County Courthouse
How 22 limestone columns, a rebellious 1960 judge, and a restored leaded glass ceiling created Kenosha’s most majestic civic sanctuary.
By Todd Burleson
November 3, 2026
Link: https://whereimaginationtakesflight.substack.com/p/field-journal-no-17-the-kenosha-county

Standing in Civic Center Park along 56th Street and looking up at the Kenosha County Courthouse, it is impossible not to feel the quiet, heavy presence of the place. Built from massive blocks of gray limestone, the three-story building is anchored by a row of 22 colossal stone columns that guard the entire city block. Carved directly into the rock high above the main doors is the building’s founding mandate. It reads, “Erected by the people of Kenosha County to the cause of just and capable government.”

I recently had the privilege of stepping inside to attend the rededication ceremony of the restored grand Ceremonial Courtroom. As I stood beneath its newly uncovered ceilings, I couldn’t help but smile to myself. Sitting in those pews for a celebration is, quite frankly, the only way I would ever want to experience that amazing space. When you stand inside that court chamber and watch sunlight stream down through glass that was hidden for over sixty years, you realize this building is not just an imposing monument of stone and mortar. It is a living vault of human drama, civic pride, and a few downright legendary local battles.

Dedicated on August 25, 1925, the “Million Dollar Courthouse” was born out of an unprecedented wave of civic reform. When it opened, visiting judges hailed it as the finest courthouse in the West. Inside, two-story grand courtrooms featured Charles Holloway murals, polished marble, and elaborate leaded glass skylights in the ceiling that flooded the chambers with natural sunlight.

For decades, court operations followed a seasonal rhythm. They wound down during the sweltering summer months because the building had no mechanical cooling. By 1960, exploding post-war court dockets made year-round sessions mandatory. That is when the Great Air Conditioning War broke out. The uncooled summer courtrooms became sweltering boxes filled with human perspiration, stale body odor, and the heavy stench of cigarette smoke.

Unbearable summer heat inside the chambers led Judge Bode to take matters into his own hands. Fed up with the stagnant air, he personally rented a window air conditioner for his chambers and mailed the invoice straight to the county. When county officials stubbornly refused to pay the bill, Judge Bode didn’t just write a letter. He held a county official in contempt of court right from the bench while court was actively in session. The feud escalated all the way to the Wisconsin Supreme Court, where Judge Bode won a decisive victory. His courtroom win forced the county to install a central cooling system. However, the ductwork technology of the 1960s required dropping the courtroom ceilings, sealing off the original ornamental plaster and hiding the grand leaded glass skylights in total darkness for the next six decades.

Taking care of the courthouse always required hands-on grit. When John R. Collins was elected County Clerk in late 1978, he took his oath of office in January 1979 and promptly learned that physical maintenance fell under his job description. Finding the 1925 landmark showing its age, Collins and his custodial crew rolled up their sleeves. They hauled heavy buckets of hot, soapy water and vinegar across the terrazzo floors. Staff spent months physically scrubbing decades of disgusting, yellowed nicotine off the plaster walls, finally revealing the original color beneath the grime. Translating my iPhone photos into flat WPA-style vector art for this project, I realize how much grueling physical labor it takes to simply stop a building from decaying.

For sixty years, the original leaded glass skylight sat forgotten above acoustic ceiling tiles. That changed when a massive preservation campaign set out to restore the Ceremonial Courtroom to its 1925 grandeur. When the renovation manager first poked his head through the dusty drop ceiling, he was stunned to see the leaded glass intact. Crews carefully removed the 1960s acoustic tiles, repaired the intricate ornamental plaster, and brought modern climate control systems in through hidden wall channels.

Standing in the court chamber today and looking up at that glowing glass ceiling, John Collins’ words at the centennial come to mind. He noted that this single room has witnessed two profound sides of the human experience. It has seen tears of sorrow during difficult sentences and legal disputes, and tears of joy during adoptions, weddings, and naturalization ceremonies for new American citizens. Restoring that skylight brings physical light back into a space where everyday citizens go to seek fairness, right wrongs, and be heard. Every one of those moments adds another layer to the building, and I would love to hear about yours.
