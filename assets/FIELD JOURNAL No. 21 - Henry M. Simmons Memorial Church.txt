FIELD JOURNAL No. 21: Henry M. Simmons Memorial Church
The Betrayal, the Books, and the Limestone Fortress
By Todd Burleson
November 17, 2026
Link: https://whereimaginationtakesflight.substack.com/p/field-journal-no-21-henry-m-simmons

When you turn onto Eighth Avenue just south of 58th Street, the rhythm of downtown Kenosha suddenly shifts gears. Most commercial buildings share the grid with polite storefront glass. This one guards the sidewalk.

Load-bearing rock: The base is constructed from a massive, rough-coursed stone foundation. Pointed Gothic arches punch through smooth cut limestone. A stout square bell tower anchors the corner. Castle-like notches crown the roofline.

It looks like a medieval fortress. But for fifty years, the only people storming this castle were Kenosha schoolkids dragging wet winter boots across the threshold. How does a Late Gothic Revival church become a reading fortress exclusively for children?

To understand that transformation, you have to look at the people who walked through these doors. In the 1870s, two men who shared a last name but zero blood shaped the city. Zalmon G. Simmons was the industrial titan behind the Simmons mattress empire. The Reverend Henry M. Simmons was a progressive Unitarian clergyman and the city’s superintendent of schools. He believed in public access to knowledge. Down in the dark basement of the congregation’s original wooden frame church, Reverend Simmons scrounged together exactly 927 volumes to start a free community lending library. Two decades later, Zalmon built the massive central library across the park. Those 927 books were hauled out of the wooden church basement to become the founding core of the entire Kenosha Public Library system.

Zalmon Simmons opened his ledger one more time. In 1907, he financed a permanent stone sanctuary on the exact footprint of the old wooden frame church to honor his late friend. Architect N. Max Dunning designed the heavy cut limestone walls and the imposing bell tower. The Reverend Florence Buck led the congregation. She was a national trailblazer and the first woman to lead a parish in Kenosha.

What nobody saw coming was the human storm brewing inside it.

Reverend Buck left in 1910. Z.G. Simmons died. The congregation started to bleed members. Kenosha’s school superintendent, Mary D. Bradford, tried to hold the line as a lay leader. Desperate for survival, the pulpit committee hired a dashing orator named Reverend James Morrison Darnell in November 1914. His sermons electrified the pews.

It lasted exactly eight weeks.

In January 1915, federal authorities stepped in. A sensational exposé revealed Darnell to be a practiced charlatan and a bigamist. They dragged him out of the pulpit and sent him to the federal penitentiary at Leavenworth. The betrayal broke the parish. Parishioners walked away in anger. The heavy wooden doors were padlocked. The massive stone building sat cold and empty for over a decade.

That is when the librarians stepped in.

In January 1928, the City of Kenosha threw the doors open again. They purchased the vacant church for $23,000 to solve a civic crisis. Across Library Park, head librarian Cora M. Frantz had ignited a radical open-stack revolution. Kenosha’s working-class families were starving for books. The main library was overflowing. Under Frantz’s command, the empty stone sanctuary was transformed into the Simmons Boys’ and Girls’ Library. Dedicated in 1929, it was the first dedicated children’s library in Wisconsin.

For the next fifty years, this space belonged strictly to the kids. I spent 14 of my 34 years in public education working as an elementary school librarian. That background completely changes how you look at a historic building. I know the physical reality of a children’s library intimately. The nave surely smelled like damp winter wool and wet books. Thousands of small hands wore the finish completely off the wooden handrails. Heavy, scuffling shoes chipped away at the floorboards and wore grooves into the marble stairs.

When the children’s library finally closed in 1979, the Gothic sanctuary drifted through private hands, most famously operating as The Landmark restaurant. But the spirit of Kenosha’s early liberal religious pioneers never truly left. In 1993, a revived local Unitarian Universalist fellowship launched a passionate $150,000 capital campaign to buy back their historic 1907 home. When they reconsecrated the limestone sanctuary, they renamed it Bradford Community Church Unitarian Universalist, honoring Mary D. Bradford, the steadfast educator and lay leader who had kept the faith alive during its darkest hour.

Kenosha lost its dedicated children's library in 1979. But the legacy refused to stay buried. In 2020, fire tore through Uptown Kenosha. The unrest left scorched earth behind. Physical grit defines this city. The community laid new mortar on 22nd Avenue. They built the Uptown Lofts directly over the scars of the riots. In July 2024, the Kenosha Public Library opened the ground-floor doors to KPL Kids. It is the city's first standalone children's library since the heavy doors on Eighth Avenue closed.

Whenever I sit down to translate a building like the old stone church into a 1930s WPA-style poster, I start by stripping away the noise. Working in flat, bold blocks of color forces you to look straight at the bones. You see the sheer physical weight of the limestone and the defiant cut of the tower. It makes you realize that survival in a city like Kenosha relies on more than load-bearing rock. From the 927 books in the basement to the children who claimed the fortress, and now the new children's library rising from the ashes of Uptown, the resilience is baked right into the stone.
