FIELD JOURNAL No. 22: St. James the Apostle Catholic Church
Frontier Solidarity, Harbor Brawls, and the Cream City Brick of 58th Street
By Todd Burleson
November 19, 2026
Link: https://whereimaginationtakesflight.substack.com/p/field-journal-no-22-st-james-the

On the southern edge of the Civic Center Historic District today, the contrast is impossible to miss. The heavy limestone courthouse guards the north end of 10th Avenue. But look south toward 58th Street, and the grid shifts. Warm, working-class Cream City brick anchors the corner. Soaring verticality: A four-story brick belfry cuts into the sky. This is St. James the Apostle Catholic Church.

That pointed tower casts a dramatic shadow over the neighborhood. It looks like a cathedral dropped right into a residential block. The cornerstone reads 1883. It was designed by John Mills Van Osdel, the same architect who pioneered clay-tile fireproofing after the Great Chicago Fire. But the physical footprint of this building hides a much older, rougher history. To understand how these cream-colored bricks got here, you have to look past the carefully painted wooden doors and imagine the mud and noise of the 1840s frontier.

Catholic life in Southport began in 1837 inside a private home. When 51 Irish immigrant families finally petitioned for a resident priest, they were broke, exhausted, and struggling to build a modest wooden church called St. Mark’s. Then came a rare moment of frontier solidarity. The city’s prominent Protestant civic leaders quietly passed around an envelope. They handed over a $400 subscription to buy the land and finish the sanctuary. There was no fanfare or glory. It was just a quiet lifeline passed to struggling laborers to help them survive.

Survival bred fierce ethnic pride. By 1851, language fractured the parish. German-speaking families broke away to form St. George, and that split bled right into the streets. Throughout the late 19th century, Irish boys from St. Mark’s and German boys from St. George regularly clashed on the harbor bridges connecting the north and south sides of town. It was typical, brutal neighborhood brawling. Scrap wood planks became weapons, and rocks and knuckles followed. It was raw energy channeling cultural pride.

That same industrial energy fueled the local saloons. Factory hands finished brutal shifts and sought the only cheap warmth they could find. The air inside those taverns was thick with stale beer, wet wool, and cheap tobacco. In 1880, a charismatic priest named Father James M. Cleary arrived to pull those men out of the smoke and brew. His devotion to the neighborhood was so absolute that the working-class Irish congregation took an extraordinary step. They petitioned the bishop to rename their 45-year-old parish in his honor. St. Mark’s officially became St. James.

Father Cleary commissioned Van Osdel to build the heavy, cream-brick structure you see today. The parish expanded into a full campus, leaning on the Dominican Sisters to educate generations of Kenosha children who would eventually build cars at Nash Motors and weave mattresses at Simmons.

When I brought this building’s photos into my digital studio to create this week’s postcard, I focused on capturing the building’s imposing silhouette and, of course, that four-story tower. Pulling a massive church into a flat, WPA-style layout changes your perspective. I had to remove a few distracting details and power lines just to focus on the sharp lines of the brickwork. It forces you to look at the sheer physical weight of the masonry. It is a brick fortress built by immigrant hands. It survived harbor brawls, saloon smoke, and a century of brutal winters to stand exactly where it belongs. Today, that working-class mission survives intact. The Downtown Kenosha Catholic Food Pantry operates right on site, serving neighborhood families directly across from the Civic Center.
