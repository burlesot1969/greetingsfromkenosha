FIELD JOURNAL No. 16: The Old Moose Lodge
Secret rituals, a crushing depression, and the heavy stone on the edge of the civic green.
By Todd Burleson
October 29, 2026
Link: https://whereimaginationtakesflight.substack.com/p/field-journal-no-16-the-old-moose

Since moving to my apartment near the harbor this past spring, my daily walks have become an exercise in the art of noticing. If you walk past the Kenosha County Courthouse on a crisp October morning, the sheer scale of the buildings surrounding Civic Center Park forces you to look up. When you reach the corner of 56th Street and 10th Avenue, the low autumn sun cuts a sharp angle across a massive, three-story block of smooth gray stone.

I am no architect. I do not know the technical names for the carved ledges or the exact quarry that produced the rock. But as a visual observer, the weight of 1010 56th Street is impossible to ignore. The visual contrast is impossible to miss. The street-facing facade is dressed in cold, flat gray stone. Walk around the corner, and it abruptly gives way to warm, textured tan brick. Up above, a row of tall, arched windows runs across the upper floors, hinting at something far grander than a typical office building. Today, quiet county employees sit behind standardized desks handling paperwork. But that modern bureaucratic silence masks a heavy story of boom, bust, and working-class survival.

Those upper floors were originally built for secrets and a fierce kind of brotherhood. Between 1926 and 1927, the Loyal Order of Moose operated as a nationwide titan. They boasted a staggering 650,000 members across more than 1,000 lodges from coast to coast. Here in Kenosha, thousands of working-class men from local factories pooled their wages. They hired architect Joseph Lindl to construct this fortress for Lodge No. 286.

It is hard to fathom today, but in the 1920s, these fraternal lodges were a matter of life and death. Before Social Security, before safety regulations, and before union contracts, a factory floor was a dangerous place. If a man was crushed by machinery or died of a sudden illness, his family faced immediate ruin. The Moose Lodge provided the ultimate safety net. It was part insurance policy and part surrogate family. They paid out sick benefits and covered funeral costs. The national organization went even further. They built and operated an entire 1,000-acre town in Illinois called Mooseheart. It featured its own schools, farms, and infrastructure designed exclusively to raise the orphaned children of deceased brothers. They even built a similar sanctuary in Florida called Moosehaven for aging members.

The Kenosha men paying dues inside 1010 56th Street were funding that massive, nationwide lifeline. They needed a towering lodge room to conduct their closed-door rituals and bind that local community together.

But the timing of this building was a tragedy. Just two years after the mortar dried, the Great Depression paralyzed the city. The roaring assembly lines at Nash Motors ground to a halt. The very men who built this luxury temple suddenly found themselves without a paycheck. Maintaining a massive limestone lodge became a crushing financial burden for a membership struggling simply to feed their families in the freezing winter.

I frame the block on my iPhone, chasing the deep shadows inside those arched windows. Later, translating the image into flat, WPA-style vector art strips away the modern signage. It leaves only the raw geometry.

The Depression's financial weight eventually broke the lodge’s grip on the building. In 1943, they sold their prized fortress to the Kenosha Labor Association. The failed luxury temple transformed into The Union Club. For the next fifty years, factory stewards and machinists packed these halls to argue over contracts, organize strikes, and fight for their livelihoods. The union had effectively taken over the very job the Moose Lodge was invented to do: protecting the working man.

Today, fraternal orders are a shadow of what they once were. Television pulled people into their living rooms, and the internet pushed us onto our screens. We look for community in digital spaces or passing interactions at local coffee shops, but we rarely build stone monuments to mutual aid anymore.

In 1994, Kenosha County purchased the property. They gutted the old union hall, broke down the massive gathering spaces, and installed cubicles. The roaring days of The Union Club and the vital safety net of the Moose are gone, but the heavy stone survives intact.

In researching this building, I learned that the architect, Joseph Lindl, had a hero-sized impact on Kenosha. He designed a portfolio of landmark buildings in Kenosha, and I am so looking forward to digging into them in future Field Journals. If you want to know a little more about where that’s headed, this Civic Center Preservation Plan from the city is a tremendous place to start!
