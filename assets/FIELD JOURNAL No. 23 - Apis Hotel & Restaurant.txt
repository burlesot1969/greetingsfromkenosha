FIELD JOURNAL No. 23: Apis Hotel & Restaurant
The Isermann Brothers, the Costume Queen, and the Open Floor Plan of 614 56th Street
By Todd Burleson
November 23, 2026
Link: https://whereimaginationtakesflight.substack.com/p/field-journal-no-23-apis-hotel-and

When I stood out on 56th Street on a quiet Monday afternoon photographing the front of Apis Hotel & Restaurant, the place was closed for the day. To anyone inside, I probably looked like a guy lingering a little too long on the sidewalk with an iPhone aimed at the windows. Then the front door cracked open. Owner Paul Campagna poked his head out, smiling, to ask if I needed help with anything.

We struck up a conversation right there on the sidewalk. When I explained the Greetings from Kenosha tour, he laughed, seemed genuinely honored to be part of it, and pointed up at the awning over the entrance, sharing a story about how his crew had to improvise to make it fit the old facade. That warmth hits you the moment you step inside, too. Grab a table on the second-floor mezzanine and look over the railing at the open middle of the dining room below, where natural light pours through the tall front windows onto the original brickwork and ornate tin ceilings. A century ago, nobody was ordering dinner up here. They were buying wool suits.

The building opened on Saturday, June 20, 1914, as Isermann Brothers Clothiers. At the time, the address was 214/216 Market Street. The sons of Anton Isermann, a German immigrant who settled in Kenosha in 1857 and raised twelve children, wanted to break away from the cramped, cluttered general stores of the nineteenth century. They commissioned a Chicago School storefront with massive front windows and an open floor plan anchored by that lofted mezzanine.

Downstairs, the National Bowling Alley and billiard hall hummed beneath the floorboards. Local ads pitched it as “A Healthy Pleasure.” On Saturday nights, when crowds spilled out of the neighboring Burke Theater one door west, the Isermann brothers kept their doors unlocked until 10:00 p.m. just to catch the late foot traffic.

The family ran the clothier for more than six decades. It survived even a Sunday morning boiler-room fire on March 20, 1955, when firefighters from Engines 1, 2, and 6 battled thick smoke that ruined thousands of dollars in spring and Easter suits. After Isermann’s finally closed in 1976, the building entered a new chapter.

Mary Kipp moved in with Furr by Us, turning the former men’s store into southeastern Wisconsin’s go-to costume shop. For nearly twenty years, local kids walked in with their grandmothers to dig through more than 3,000 outfits, from Santa suits to werewolf masks, before Kipp closed up in 1993 to work the Bristol Renaissance Faire. Wilmot’s Rebuilt Washers had a brief run in 1996, and then the doors locked for roughly two decades.

When Paul Campagna bought the vacant building in 2017, he saw what most people missed. A former house flipper who caught the hospitality bug after running Pazzo with his brother Brian, Campagna partnered with his fiancée Kristine under the banner Grease & Honey, a nod to the elbow grease of fixing up old buildings and the honey of sweet success. Inside 614 56th Street, the past had never been packed up. Campagna had to host an estate sale just to clear out 15,000 square feet of left-behind sewing patterns, record players, Greek vinyl records, and thousands of beads.

Walking the second floor, which had been used as a warehouse for decades, he spotted the layout of old offices and realized the space could become boutique suites. Between 2017 and 2021, Campagna and his team restored the historic brick and tin ceilings while turning the building into four levels of food and drink, including a moody basement lounge where bowlers once rolled strikes, six second-floor guest suites, and a rooftop bar looking out over downtown and Lake Michigan.

He named the flagship Apis, the Latin word for bee, crowning a downtown family of spots alongside The Buzz Café and its next-door neighbor, the former Sazzy B, which is currently closed for renovations as the team prepares its next chapter. Talking with Campagna on the sidewalk outside on a closed Monday, it is easy to see why this place works. He has the exact kind of generosity and pride in downtown you hope to find in the people building Kenosha’s gathering spaces, keeping a 1914 storefront alive for another century.
