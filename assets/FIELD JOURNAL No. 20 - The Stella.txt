FIELD JOURNAL No. 20: The Stella
The 100-Year Phoenix: The Malt House Ashes and the Elks Club Rescue
By Todd Burleson
November 12, 2026
Link: https://whereimaginationtakesflight.substack.com/p/field-journal-no-20-the-stella

Morning traffic on 8th Avenue moves right past the Stella Hotel without noticing its sheer physical weight. Load-bearing geometry: Towering white columns and a massive brick facade built to last. Today, out-of-town guests cross those polished floors for a quiet drink. A century ago, this was a completely different world. This building was not made for luxury. Local factory workers built it. It survived frozen pipes, a massive fire, and a city demolition crew.

Long before the red brick went up, this exact parcel was a scorched hole in the ground. It originally housed the Pettit Malting Company. As the oldest malt house in Wisconsin, it cranked out 600,000 bushels of malt annually for Chicago and Milwaukee breweries. On St. Patrick’s Day morning in 1914, a catastrophic fire swept through the three-story facility. The blaze burned for days. It left the site completely leveled.

From those charred ashes emerged 300 local members of the Benevolent and Protective Order of Elks. In June 1916, they pooled $65,000 to buy the vacant lot. With mattress titan Zalmon G. Simmons guiding the building committee, the lodge commissioned Milwaukee architect Robert Messmer to design a monumental four-story Georgian Revival structure of brick and limestone. The clubhouse was formally dedicated in January 1919. Ten years later, those same local members threw a massive celebration and literally burned the building’s mortgage document in triumph.

Think about the men walking through those heavy doors. Machinists from Nash Motors and brass workers stepped off grueling shifts and entered a palace. They shared a roof with visiting elites, eventually watching jazz legend Mel Tormé perform under the ballroom chandeliers. Distilling this building into flat color blocks reveals its true visual DNA. Translating the sharp architectural lines of the dining halls into saturated shapes on my screen forces you to notice the physical comfort this space offered. The smooth marble, warm brick, and heavy timber provided a working-class sanctuary for rough, calloused hands.

Buildings decay when a community shifts. By the early 1990s, the Elks sold the property. Renamed the Heritage House Inn, the structure entered a long period of neglect. Absentee owners left the municipal water supply on during a harsh winter. Main pipes froze and burst. Thousands of gallons of freezing water poured through the ceiling joints. Local philanthropist Andrea Christensen stepped in, spending heavily to repair the flooded structure for banquet use. By the mid-2000s, the heavy doors locked again. The vacant landmark deteriorated into an urban shell. The grand ballroom where Mel Tormé once sang became a freezing, makeshift haven for Kenosha’s homeless community trying to survive the bitter lake winds.

The breaking point arrived on October 28, 2011. A passing firefighter spotted thick smoke billowing from the upper windows. Fire departments battled a blaze trapped deep between the heavy timber supports and the multi-layered floorboards of the second-floor ballroom. Two months later, the City of Kenosha issued a formal raze order.

Demolition seemed inevitable until a grassroots citizen coalition called “Preserve the Elks” packed municipal halls in early 2012. Kenoshans fight to preserve the past because our physical memories anchor us to the grid. The citizens secured a 120-day delay from the bulldozers. That stand saved the heavy masonry. In 2018, a multi-million-dollar restoration began. Crews exposed the original interior brickwork, repaired the fire-scarred floorboards, and meticulously restored the original craftsmanship. The heavy doors finally unlocked in 2019, bringing the building back to life as The Stella Hotel. The site is now a massive hospitality hub containing 80 guest rooms, divided into 12 Heritage Suites and 68 standard rooms. The updated footprint also holds a café, a supper-club-inspired dining room, and a seasonal rooftop bar.

During the recent Keys to Kenosha event, I walked through the building to see the restoration firsthand. If you go looking for the old Elks swimming pool today, you will not find any water. The basin is covered over and serves as a modern boardroom, though the original perimeter tiles survived the renovation intact. I also tried to get a look at the grand ballroom, but the main doors were closed to prep for a wedding. The Stella has become the hottest wedding hub in the city. I snuck upstairs anyway. Standing on the beautiful balcony overlooking the 4,000-square-foot ballroom floor, I captured a few photos of the restored antique chandeliers. Looking down at that massive space, the sheer scale is magnificent. It is easy to stand up there and imagine exactly what it looked like when Mel Tormé performed for a packed house.
